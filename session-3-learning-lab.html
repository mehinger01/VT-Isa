Step 1

The three-part learning system

Fast courses become difficult when class is the first time you see an idea and the last time you actively work with it. The solution is to spread learning across three short moments.

Before class

Build a small mental map and identify what to listen for.

During class

Capture the structure of the lesson instead of every word.

After class

Retrieve from memory, check gaps, and create precise questions.

The goal

Not more studying. Better timing and better mental work.

Step 2

Before class: Preview with a purpose

A preview should take about four minutes. Its purpose is not to teach the lesson. It prepares your attention.

1

Scan

Look at the title, headings, bold terms, diagrams, formulas, examples, or assigned questions.

2

Connect

Ask: What do I already know that may connect to this?

3

Question

Write one question you want the class to answer.

Before-class rule: do not try to master everything. Enter class with a map and a question.
Example: Before a history lesson, scan the section headings, notice the dates and people, connect them to the previous unit, and ask, “What caused this conflict to escalate?”
Avoid: reading every line, copying definitions, or turning the preview into a full study session.

Step 3

During class: Capture structure, not a transcript

Your notes should help you think later. Write less, but capture what gives the lesson meaning.

The five things worth capturing

  1. Main idea: What is the teacher trying to show?
  2. How or why: What mechanism, reasoning, or cause explains it?
  3. Example: What example makes it concrete?
  4. Contrast or exception: When is it different, limited, or easy to confuse?
  5. Question: What is still unresolved?
During-class rule: when you feel behind, stop chasing sentences and return to the main idea.
Useful shorthand: ✓ clear   ? uncertain   ! important   → causes/leads to   vs. contrast
Avoid: copying slides word for word, making notes decorative during class, or assuming complete notes equal understanding.

Step 4

After class: Retrieve, check, and repair

Do not begin by rereading. First find out what you can produce without the notes.

1

Close

Close the notes and source material.

2

Retrieve

Explain the lesson aloud or on paper in your own words. Reproduce the major steps, relationships, diagram, or argument.

3

Check

Open the notes and compare. Mark what was missing, vague, or incorrect.

4

Repair

Correct the gap, then try that part again without looking.

5

Ask

Turn any remaining confusion into a precise teacher question.

After-class rule: memory first, notes second.
Strong teacher question: “I understand the first two steps, but I lose the reasoning when the author moves from evidence to the conclusion. What connection am I missing?”
Avoid: rereading until the page feels familiar, copying notes neatly, or checking the answer after only a few seconds.

Step 5

Build the version you will actually use

The best method is one you can repeat. Create both a full version and a minimum version for busy days.

Full version

Minimum version for busy days

Before: one question.
During: main idea, one example, one question.
After: one closed-note explanation, then check.

One-week test

Use the routine in one real learning situation. Notice whether class feels easier to follow and whether you can explain more afterward.
