Session 1 · Learning Methods Toolkit

Use the right kind of practice.

Learn six high-value learning methods, when each one works, and how to avoid the study habits that feel productive without producing strong learning.

Step 1

Learning is not the same as exposure

Seeing, hearing, highlighting, or rereading information can make it feel familiar. Strong learning requires producing, connecting, applying, and correcting information.

The central rule

Do not ask, “Did I look at it?” Ask, “What can I produce without help?”

Low-value alone

Rereading, highlighting, copying notes, watching examples, and doing many identical problems.

High-value mental work

Retrieving, explaining, spacing, comparing, applying, receiving feedback, and retrying.

Method 1

Retrieval practice

Pull information out of memory before looking at the source. Retrieval strengthens access and exposes what is missing.

Best for recall, explanation, and checking mastery

How to do it

  1. Close the notes, book, or video.
  2. Write, say, draw, or solve everything you can remember.
  3. Check the source.
  4. Correct the gap.
  5. Close the source and try the missed part again.
Examples: blank-page recall, practice questions, flashcards requiring production, explaining a process aloud, redrawing a diagram from memory.
Avoid: flipping a flashcard and thinking “I knew that,” rereading before trying, or checking after only a few seconds.

Method 2

Spacing

Revisit learning across multiple days instead of concentrating all practice into one sitting. Some forgetting between sessions makes retrieval more useful.

Best for long-term retention

A simple schedule

  • First retrieval: later the same day or the next day
  • Second retrieval: 2–3 days later
  • Third retrieval: about one week later
  • Continue spacing farther apart when recall remains strong
Use it with: vocabulary, formulas, concepts, processes, quotations, diagrams, and cumulative problem solving.
Avoid: repeating the same material many times in one night and assuming the resulting fluency will last.

Method 3

Self-explanation and deep questions

Explain why an idea works, how it connects, and when it applies. This turns disconnected facts into a usable mental model.

Best for concepts and reasoning

Questions that create understanding

  • What is the main claim?
  • Why is it true?
  • How does it work?
  • What does it connect to?
  • What is an example and a non-example?
  • When would it not apply?
Strong explanation: claim + mechanism + connection + example + boundary.
Avoid: vague “teach it to a child” explanations that remove the precise vocabulary or reasoning the subject requires.

Method 4

Worked examples with fading

When a procedure is new, study a correct model first. Then remove support gradually until you can perform independently.

Best for new procedures and complex problems

The fading sequence

  1. Study a complete worked example and explain each step.
  2. Complete an example with the final steps removed.
  3. Complete one with more steps removed.
  4. Solve a new problem independently.
  5. Compare and correct.
Use it for: mathematical procedures, chemistry calculations, grammar analysis, research citations, lab processes, and structured writing.
Avoid: watching examples passively or jumping immediately into unsupported problem solving when the procedure is still unfamiliar.

Methods 5–6

Interleaving, feedback, and correction

Mix related problem types when you need to choose a method, and use feedback to correct the rule—not merely the final answer.

Interleaving

Mix related types

Practice several related categories in mixed order so you must decide what kind of problem or situation you are facing.

Best for: method selection, distinguishing similar concepts, and transfer.

Avoid: doing ten identical problems and mistaking repetition for the ability to choose independently.
Feedback and correction

Repair the model

Identify what was wrong, why it was wrong, the cue you missed, and then attempt a new variation later.

Best for: preventing repeated errors.

Avoid: reading the correct answer once, writing it into an error log, and never retesting it.

Final step

Match the method to the learning demand

No single strategy is best for every task. Diagnose what the course requires, then choose the mental work that matches it.
