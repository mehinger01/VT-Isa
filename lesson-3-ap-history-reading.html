New APUSH Practice Reading · Period 6

Industrialization and the Remaking of American Society, 1865–1900

Between the end of the Civil War and the beginning of the twentieth century, the United States became one of the world’s leading industrial powers. Railroads connected national markets, corporations reorganized production, and inventions accelerated communication and manufacturing. This expansion created enormous wealth and widened access to consumer goods, but it also concentrated economic power, disrupted older ways of life, and exposed sharp inequalities in American society.

Railroad construction provided the physical framework for national industrial growth. Federal land grants and private investment supported thousands of miles of track, linking farms, mines, factories, and ports. Railroads increased demand for steel, coal, lumber, and machinery while allowing businesses to sell goods across greater distances. Standardized time zones and more predictable shipping schedules also changed how Americans organized work and daily life. Yet railroad companies wielded tremendous influence over prices and politics, leading many farmers and small businesses to accuse them of discriminatory practices.

New forms of corporate organization transformed production. Business leaders such as Andrew Carnegie used vertical integration to control multiple stages of an industry, while John D. Rockefeller’s Standard Oil pursued horizontal integration by absorbing or eliminating competitors. Supporters argued that large corporations lowered prices, increased efficiency, and made possible projects that smaller firms could not finance. Critics countered that trusts restricted competition, manipulated markets, and allowed a small number of executives to exercise power that rivaled that of elected governments.

Industrial growth depended heavily on a rapidly expanding labor force. Millions of immigrants from southern and eastern Europe entered the United States, while rural Americans and African Americans seeking opportunity moved toward growing cities. Factory wages offered a path into the cash economy, but work was often dangerous, repetitive, and poorly paid. Twelve-hour shifts, child labor, industrial accidents, and abrupt layoffs were common. Workers who organized unions or strikes faced employers, private security forces, courts, and at times state or federal troops.

The conflict between labor and capital became visible in events such as the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Haymarket affair of 1886, and the Homestead Strike of 1892. These confrontations revealed both the desperation of workers and the limited public support available to organized labor. Many middle-class Americans feared that strikes threatened social order, while workers believed that corporations and government institutions routinely protected property over people. Labor organizations nevertheless helped establish the principle that industrial democracy required collective bargaining, not merely individual effort.

Industrialization also reshaped American cities. Urban populations grew rapidly as factories attracted workers and immigrants formed ethnic neighborhoods near jobs and transportation. Cities became centers of culture, commerce, and political influence, but housing and public services often failed to keep pace. Tenements, pollution, disease, and overcrowding became defining features of working-class life. Political machines sometimes exploited immigrant voters, yet they also supplied jobs, food, legal assistance, and other services that formal government structures failed to provide.

The benefits and burdens of industrialization were distributed unevenly. A growing middle class enjoyed department stores, mail-order catalogs, electric lighting, and new forms of entertainment. Wealthy industrialists built fortunes on a scale previously unknown in the United States. At the same time, many workers remained economically insecure, Indigenous communities lost land to western development, and African Americans faced segregation, violence, and exclusion from many of the period’s expanding opportunities. Economic growth therefore did not erase older hierarchies; in many cases, it adapted and reinforced them.

By 1900, industrialization had permanently altered the nation’s economy and social structure. It expanded productive capacity, connected distant regions, and created new opportunities for mobility and innovation. It also generated concentrated corporate power, unstable labor conditions, and crowded urban environments that demanded political reform. The central question of the era was no longer whether the United States would industrialize, but whether democratic institutions could govern the economic forces industrialization had unleashed.
